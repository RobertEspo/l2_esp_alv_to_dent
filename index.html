Methods

Visual Feedback

Speakers incorporate visual cues in their L1 (Bernstein et al., 2004) and L2 (Engwall, 2008; Navarra & Soto-Faraco, 2007)

L2 classrooms typically avoid teaching pronunciation (Derwing & Munro, 2005; Gilbert & Grant, 2014) despite the benefits (Gordon et al., 2012; Saito, 2015)

Suprasegmentals have been taught with visual feedback (Chun, 1989, 1998, 2002)

  • … but little has been done with segmentals (Bliss et al., 2018)

Indirect visual feedback includes e.g., spectrography

Spectrographic feedback resulted in improvements for vowels and consonants (Akahane et al., 1997; Molholt, 1990; Niermann, 2016; Olson, 2014)

but…

  • steep learning curve
  • does not provide an explanation of the user’s error
  • or the articulatory cause of those errors (Rogerson-Revell, 2021; Wilson, 2014)

Direct visual feedback provides information about articulators

Electromagnetic articulography (EMA) has produced gains in learner pronunciation of segments (Levitt & Katz, 2007; Suemitsu et al., 2015)

… but EMA is prohibitively expensive for the teacher and uncomfortably intrusive for the learner

Ultrasound machines have become increasingly affordable, portable, and are non-invasive (Gick et al., 2005)

History of use in…

  • clinical setting (Dugan et al., 2023)
  • teaching spoken language to the hard-of-hearing and deaf (Bernhardt et al., 2003, 2005)
  • accent correction in L2 learners (Badin et al., 2010; Ouni, 2014)

Despite benefits, remains infrequently used in SLA research and the L2 classroom

Common to investigate L2 acquisition of VOT (Casillas, 2020b; Olson, 2022; Schuhmann & Huffman, 2019)

Less common to investigate acquisition of place of articulation

American English speakers can distinguish alveolar vs dental stops in Irish English with only slightly less accuracy than Irish English natives (Kelly, 2019)

Rhotics

[ɾ] is phonemic in Spanish and allophonic in English, but they’re phonetically similar (Reetz & Jongman, 2020; Zue & Laferriere, 1979)

Despite that, beginner L1 English L2 Spanish produce [ɾ] as [ɹ] (Weech, 2009)

Overall target-like production increases with proficiency (Face, 2006) and experience (Weech, 2009)

But even 5th semester undergrads and graduate students in a Hispanic linguistics & literature program found that learners do not encode /ɾ r/ distinction in their lexicon (Daidone & Darcy, 2014)

Coronal Stop-Rhotic Clusters

/t d/ + rhotic onset clusters are phonotactically legal in both English & Spanish

Although the individual segments are relatively similar, the clusters are realized phonetically differently

American English /tr dr/ are often pronounced as rhotacized post-alveolar affricates (Carley & Mees, 2019; Cruttenden, 2014; Schwartz, 2023; Scobbie et al., 2006)

Spanish /tɾ dɾ/ are often pronounced [tɾ dɾ]

Spanish Coronal Stop-Rhotic Clusters

L1 English L2 Spanish /tɾ dɾ/ production has been assessed with five measures (Colantoni & Steele, 2006, 2018; González & Weissglass, 2023; Morales, 2017):

  • stop VOT
  • rhotic manner
  • rhotic duration
  • presence of epenthetic vowel
  • duration of epenthetic vowel

Learners who receive spectrography training outperform control group on target-like production (Morales, 2017)

Target-like production does occur in advanced learners, but it’s the exception (Colantoni & Steele, 2006)

L1 English L2 Spanish acquire coronal stop-rhotic cluster phonetics in specific order:


stop voicing > rhotic manner/duration >

presence/absence & duration of epenthetic vowel

Is this ordering sensitive to training?

Rhotic manner and presence of epenthetic vowel seem dependent on POA of stop, but little explicit or direct investigation of stop placement in L1 English L2 Spanish learners

(Colantoni & Steele, 2006; Morales, 2017)

Perception-Production Link

L2LP (Van Leussen & Escudero, 2015) proposes perception precedes production (Casillas, 2020a), so it seems unintuitive to train production before perception

but…

  • we see that production can precede perception (Sheldon & Strange, 1982)
  • some L2 models privilege the role of production (Swain, 2005)
  • learners given articulatory instruction, instead of just “listen and repeat”, perform better in production & perception (Catford & Pisoni, 1970)

Research Questions

  1. Does training coronal stop-rhotic clusters in L1 English L2 Spanish learners speed up acquisition?

  2. Is the order of the phonetic aspects of coronal stop-rhotic clusters fixed for L1 English L2 Spanish learners?

  3. Does perception of phonetic aspects of coronal stop-rhotic clusters precede production in L1 English L2 Spanish learners?

Methods

Participants

  • 20 L1 English L2 Spanish learners
    • 10 in experimental group
    • 10 in control group
  • Proficiency level: A1
  • Domestic immersion program

Tasks

  • Delayed shadowing (production)
  • AXB discrimination (perception)
  • AB identification (perception)

Delayed Shadowing

  • 30 target nonce words & 40 distractors
  • target words: bisyllabic trVCV with penultimate stress
  • each of five Spanish vowels represented first V six times
  • Second syllable variably started with one of six stop consonants
  • Final V always /a/
  • Presented in random order & repeated three times each
  • Recorded with ultrasound imaging

AXB Discrimination & AB Identification

  • Stimuli manipulated on two dimensions: place of articulation and voicing
  • POA differences produced by native bilingual
  • VOT differences manipulated in Praat

Ultrasound Training

  • 30 to 45 mins
  • Participants given ~10 mins of instructions during first training session
  • Shown how to view midsaggital or coronal views
  • Provided with .mp4 files of native Spanish speakers producing target clusters with time aligned audio and text
  • Provided with the following questions at each session (Olson, 2014):
    • Describe the visual difference between your production and pronunciation by a native Spanish speaker
    • Where is the Spanish speaker’s tongue tip when they pronounce ‘t’?
    • Where is the Spanish speaker’s tongue tip when they pronounce ‘r’?

Procedure

Five sessions over five consecutive weeks

At each session…

Control group completed delayed shadowing -> AXB discrimination -> AB identification

  • Experimental group completed delayed shadowing –> AXB discrimination –> AB identification

  • At sessions 2, 3, and 4, they also completed 30-45 mins of ultrasound training

Data Analysis

  • Acoustic and perception judgements over the 5 sessions will be analyzed using generalized additive linear models
  • “Magic moment” of ultrasound will be tongue tip contact for stop consonant
  • Or looking for two points of contact (dental stop + alveolar tap)?
  • Advice would be appreciated :-)
